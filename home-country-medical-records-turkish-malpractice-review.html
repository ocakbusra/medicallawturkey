Can Medical Records From My Home Country Support a Case in Türkiye?

How to organise records from corrective or follow-up care abroad for a legal review involving treatment in Türkiye. Estimated reading time: 8–10 minutes

Short answer

Records created in your home country may help explain later treatment, symptoms and the timeline after care in Türkiye. Preserve the complete source documents, identify who created each one, and organise a translation plan. A Turkish civil court may require a Turkish translation of foreign-language documents and can request an official translation.

Updated: 25 September 2026. This is general legal information, not advice on an individual matter.

Why later records may matter

If you sought follow-up or corrective treatment after returning home, those records can help a reviewer understand what happened next. They may document your reported symptoms, examination findings, diagnosis, proposed treatment and the date of later care. They do not automatically prove what occurred in Türkiye or why a particular result developed.

For a useful review, connect each later record to the earlier treatment. Identify the clinician, specialty, visit date and question addressed. Separate what you personally reported from what the clinician observed or concluded.

Keep medical care decisions with your treating clinicians. Do not delay a recommended assessment or procedure to create a legal file. Ask the local provider for records when appropriate and focus on accurate documentation of care actually received.

Build a complete record set

Ask the later provider for the parts of the file relevant to the issue under review. Depending on the care, that may include:

  • Consultation and examination notes, including the date and clinician’s specialty.
  • Imaging, laboratory results and diagnostic reports in the original format.
  • Procedure reports, consent documents and discharge instructions for revision or corrective treatment.
  • Prescriptions, medication records, referrals and follow-up notes.
  • Invoices or proof of expense if a financial loss may be relevant.
  • Photographs taken as part of clinical care, with dates and context where available.

Request the records directly from the provider that created them. Keep the unaltered files and a separate working index. If a note is corrected later, retain both the original and the correction history where available.

Prepare a chronology that connects the providers

Make a one-page timeline with the date of the Türkiye procedure, the first symptom or concern, the date you contacted the clinic, each later appointment and each treatment decision. Use exact dates where you can; mark estimates as estimates. Add the document that supports each entry.

For each later appointment, note what the clinician was asked to assess and whether the visit addressed diagnosis, urgent care, revision options or a second opinion. Do not paraphrase a clinical conclusion in a stronger way than the report uses. If the report is unclear, ask the authoring clinic for clarification.

A medical reviewer may need to distinguish a pre-existing condition, a known risk, a later independent event and a possible treatment-related issue. A clear chronology helps frame those questions but cannot answer them by itself.

Translation and authentication questions

Preserve documents in their original language. For a preliminary discussion, a working translation may help identify key information. If a party relies on a foreign-language document in Turkish civil proceedings, Article 223 of the Code of Civil Procedure requires a translation to be submitted, and the court may also request an official translation.

That rule does not mean every document needs the same translator or authentication step in every situation. The appropriate translation, certification or authentication can depend on the document, country of origin, purpose and court direction. Before paying to translate a large file, ask counsel which records are material and what form is needed.

Keep the translator’s name, date and version with the translation. Do not discard the source-language file, and do not translate only selected sentences without preserving the full page and context.

How a legal review can use the records

A Turkish lawyer can help identify which records relate to the alleged act, the harm and the timeline. A medical expert may need the underlying clinical material, not only a brief letter written for legal purposes. The review may also need the Turkish provider’s own file, the consent materials and communications before and after treatment.

Send documents through a secure channel agreed with the legal team. Health information is sensitive personal data. Share only what is needed for the requested review and ask how it will be stored or transmitted.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a Turkish case include records from another country?

They can be relevant to a review, especially for later care and chronology. Their use and evidentiary weight depend on the case and applicable procedure.

Do I need an official translation of every document?

Not necessarily for an initial review. Article 223 addresses foreign-language documents in civil proceedings, and a court may request an official translation. Confirm requirements before commissioning translations.

Do I need an apostille?

That depends on the country, document type and intended use. Do not assume one rule applies to every medical record; obtain case-specific guidance.

Should I wait for a legal review before getting revision treatment?

No legal article should replace clinical advice. Prioritise appropriate medical care and keep records of decisions and treatment.
