A Different Surgeon Performed My Procedure in Türkiye: What Should I Check?

How to document a difference between the surgeon you expected and the clinician recorded in your Turkish cosmetic-surgery file. Estimated reading time: 7–9 minutes

Short answer

First establish exactly who assessed and performed each part of the procedure. Compare booking and clinic communications with the consent form, operation report, discharge documents and invoice. A name mismatch deserves clarification, but it does not alone prove negligence or determine legal responsibility.

Updated: 25 September 2026. This is general legal information, not advice on an individual matter.

Start by identifying the people and roles

International patients may communicate with a coordinator, book through an intermediary, meet one clinician and receive care from a team. A difference between the name used in marketing or booking and a name in the medical file can therefore have several explanations. The legal and clinical significance depends on who made the treatment decisions, who performed each step, what the patient was told and what the records show.

Do not rely on a social-media profile, first name or clinic brand to identify the responsible provider. Make a list of every person and organisation mentioned in your records, with the role attributed to each one and the document where that information appears.

The Patient Rights Regulation says that information before an intervention includes who will perform it, where and how it will be performed, and its expected duration. It also provides for access to records. Use those provisions as a basis for asking the provider to clarify the treatment record, not as a shortcut to a conclusion about fault.

Compare the documents side by side

  • Booking and consultation: save the original proposal, surgeon profile, appointment confirmation and messages describing who would treat you.
  • Consent and patient information: note the clinician or team named, the procedure described, the date and the language of the form.
  • Operation or procedure report: check the name and role recorded for the primary operator and any assistants, along with the date and facility.
  • Discharge and follow-up: identify who gave instructions, who was listed as your treating clinician and how to contact them.
  • Invoice and contract: record the legal entity that received payment and whether a separate intermediary appears.
  • Later explanations: preserve written answers explaining a change in provider, procedure plan or team.

Keep the files as received. If a spelling differs between transliteration and a passport or licence, note the discrepancy without changing the original document.

Questions to put to the provider

A calm, specific request is more useful than an accusation. Ask the clinic or hospital to confirm in writing:

  • Which clinician assessed you before the procedure?
  • Who performed each relevant part, and what role did each team member have?
  • Was the planned provider changed? If so, when was the change made and how was it explained?
  • Where are the complete consent and procedure records held?
  • Which legal entity provided the clinical service, and which entity handled booking or payment?

Request missing records through the provider’s verified patient-record channel. Article 16 of the Patient Rights Regulation refers to inspecting and obtaining copies of the patient’s health records. See our practical medical-record request template.

What the name difference can and cannot show

A name difference may be relevant to questions about information, consent, contract terms or the clinical record. It does not, by itself, prove that the treatment was below an appropriate standard, that consent was invalid, or that a particular entity is legally responsible. Those questions require a fact-specific review of the full record and applicable rules.

Similarly, a clinician’s name appearing in an advertisement does not establish that the person promised a particular outcome or personally performed every step. Preserve the exact source and ask a reviewer to compare it with the actual treatment documents.

If your concern relates to a specific outcome, explain the medical issue separately from the identity discrepancy. For example, describe the date of the procedure, the later symptoms or findings, the advice you received and any subsequent treatment. A clinician should address current health needs; a lawyer can assess the legal questions.

Prepare a compact identity-and-role table

For an initial legal review, create four columns: person or company name; stated role; where you saw the name; and what needs clarification. Attach the underlying document rather than relying on memory. Keep personal identifiers out of any public post or unverified channel.

If you have already received an explanation, add its date and preserve the original message. Do not ask staff to alter a clinical record; if a record contains a factual error, make a separate written request for clarification or correction and keep the response.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a different surgeon’s name prove malpractice?

No. It is a fact to clarify. Clinical standard, consent, causation, loss and the roles of each provider require separate assessment.

Which record is most useful for identifying the surgeon?

Compare the consent documents, procedure or operation report, discharge records and booking communications. No single document necessarily answers every role question.

Can I ask the clinic why the surgeon changed?

Yes. A specific written question can help establish what happened and when. Keep a copy of the request and reply.

Who is responsible if an intermediary arranged the surgery?

That depends on what each provider agreed to do and did in practice. Do not assume that a brand name or booking platform identifies every legally relevant party.
