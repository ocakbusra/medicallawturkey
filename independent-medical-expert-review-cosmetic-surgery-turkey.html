What Does an Independent Medical Expert Need to Review a Cosmetic-Surgery Case in Türkiye?

Prepare a clear, complete file for an independent medical review after cosmetic surgery in Türkiye, and understand what an expert opinion can and cannot decide. Estimated reading time: 8–10 minutes

Short answer

An independent medical review is most useful when the reviewer receives the relevant clinical records, a dated treatment chronology, the specific medical questions and records of later care. A medical opinion can help explain technical issues; it does not by itself decide legal liability, compensation or the outcome of a court case.

Updated: 25 September 2026. This is general legal information, not advice on an individual matter.

Medical questions and legal questions are different

A cosmetic-surgery dispute may involve technical questions about the planned procedure, the care delivered, a complication, follow-up or the cause of a later condition. A clinician with relevant expertise can help explain those medical issues. A lawyer assesses how the evidence fits legal requirements. A court determines issues within the case before it.

Under Article 266 of the Code of Civil Procedure, a court may seek expert assistance when resolving a matter requires knowledge outside ordinary or legal knowledge. A private independent review requested before or outside litigation is not the same thing as a court-appointed expert process.

Neither a private opinion nor a court expert’s report guarantees a finding in your favour. The full record, other evidence, objections and the court’s assessment can matter.

What to send for a focused review

  • Procedure records: pre-operative assessment, consent and information documents, operation report, anaesthesia and recovery records, discharge instructions.
  • Follow-up: clinic messages, appointments, referrals, prescriptions and any later examination or corrective treatment.
  • Chronology: procedure date, when the concern appeared, who was contacted, what advice was given and when later care occurred.
  • Images and tests: original clinical photographs or imaging with dates and context; avoid edited or selectively cropped versions.
  • Booking and payment: treatment proposal, contract, invoices, provider identity and any refund or revision offer.
  • Specific questions: a short list of issues you want the reviewer to explain, rather than a request for a guaranteed legal outcome.

Keep documents in their original format and identify the source. If records are in different languages, label the language and ask whether a working translation is enough for the first review.

Frame questions neutrally

Questions should help the reviewer examine the records rather than assume the conclusion. Examples include:

  • What procedure is documented, and what clinical findings were recorded before it?
  • What do the operation and follow-up records say was performed?
  • Which risks or alternatives are documented in the patient-information materials?
  • What do later records describe, and what additional clinical information would be needed to assess them?
  • Can the available records support a medical explanation for the outcome, or are important records missing?

A lawyer can help separate questions for a clinician from questions about legal responsibility, limitation periods, parties or recoverable loss. Avoid asking a medical expert to decide legal liability unless the applicable procedure specifically asks for a defined technical opinion.

Choose the right reviewer and protect the file

The relevant expertise depends on the procedure and issue. A reviewer should be able to understand the type of treatment involved and should explain the limits of the material they received. Ask who will review the file, what specialty or experience is relevant, whether the reviewer has any connection to a party, what the fee covers and whether a written opinion is included.

Do not send an unredacted file through a public comment, an unverified email address or a general contact form. Confirm a secure document-sharing route. Ask whether the reviewer needs identity details, and share only what is necessary for the assessment.

Where an independent clinician identifies missing information, request it from the provider before drawing conclusions. A short extract may not show the clinical context, and a translated summary may omit details present in the source record.

What happens after the review?

Use the opinion as one input in the next decision. It may identify a need for more records, another specialty, a clearer timeline or a legal consultation. It may also conclude that the available information cannot answer the question. Both outcomes can be useful because they make uncertainty visible.

If litigation begins, the court may use its own expert procedure. The parties may have opportunities to submit questions or objections under the applicable procedural rules. The precise steps depend on the case and should be explained by counsel.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an independent medical opinion the same as a court expert report?

No. A private opinion and a court-appointed expert process are different. Their roles and weight depend on the procedural context.

Can an expert say whether I will win?

A medical reviewer can address technical medical questions within their expertise, but cannot guarantee what a court will decide.

What if I do not have every medical record?

Provide an index of what you have and clearly list what is missing. Ask the reviewer or lawyer whether to request more records before reaching a view.

Should I ask for a review before filing a claim?

That can be one option, but timing and necessity are case-specific. A lawyer can explain the available review routes and any deadlines.
